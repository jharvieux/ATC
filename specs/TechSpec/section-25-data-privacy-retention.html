25.4 Anonymization of Required-Retention Data ¶

Booking and commission records are subject to 7-year retention; they survive customer deletion via anonymization:

  • Customer name replaced with hash-derived placeholder
  • Customer email cleared; reference to deleted user only
  • Customer DOB cleared (no longer needed post-sailing for retention purpose)
  • Booking financial and audit details preserved

25.4a Free-Text Anonymization Strategy ¶

Free-text fields (chat messages, conversation notes, AI-generated narratives) cannot be reliably anonymized in place — PII redaction on years-old free-text via LLM is expensive, slow, and produces false negatives. The platform takes a different approach for the two categories of free-text data:

Category 1: Conversation messages (messages.content)

On customer deletion after the 30-day grace period:

  • messages.content set to NULL. The free-text body is removed entirely.
  • Message metadata retained. id, conversation_id, sender_role, created_at, token counts, supervisor_findings JSONB (with any PII fields within it nulled separately) all stay.
  • Conversation continuity preserved for the tenant. The tenant can still see "conversation existed with N messages of which K were customer-sent" for their own historical analytics, but cannot read the customer's words.

This is deletion, not anonymization. The body is gone.

If a tenant needs to preserve conversation content for legal-hold purposes (active dispute), the forensics log per §26.5a captures a snapshot BEFORE the deletion. The snapshot is encrypted and access-controlled per that section; it is NOT in the tenant's normal operational view.

Category 2: AI-generated narratives in other tables

Some tables contain AI-generated free-text:

  • quotes.narrative — generated quote description
  • bookings.notes — generated booking summary
  • customer_memories.notes_freeform — extracted free-form notes

On customer deletion:

  • customer_memories row is hard-deleted entirely (the row exists to track customer state; if there's no customer, there's no row).
  • quotes.narrative and bookings.notes: NULLed for the affected customer's records. The booking record itself is retained per §25.4 (financial retention) but the narrative is gone.

Category 3: Tenant-owned content referencing the customer

Tenant-authored notes (CRM notes typed by a tenant agent about a customer) are tenant-owned. On customer deletion:

  • The notes are NOT auto-deleted. The tenant agent wrote them; the tenant retains them as part of their business records.
  • The notes' references to the customer (foreign keys) are replaced with the same hash-derived placeholder from §25.4.
  • The text body of the notes is retained but the tenant is encouraged (via UI) to review and redact any PII manually.

Why deletion (not redaction) for messages.content

Three reasons:

  1. Reliable PII redaction on years-old free-text is hard. Names, addresses, phone numbers, casual references — an LLM pass produces false negatives. Deletion is reliable.
  2. The customer's intent on deletion is clear. They want their data gone. Retaining redacted versions of their messages doesn't serve them.
  3. Cost. Running an LLM redaction pass over the message history of every CCPA-deleted customer is real Anthropic spend.

The trade-off: the tenant loses the ability to read historical conversations with terminated customers. This is the right default for CCPA compliance.

Calls Worth Flagging

  • Forensics-log snapshot before deletion is the recovery path for active disputes. A customer who is mid-dispute deletes their account, then resumes the dispute later: the snapshot lets the platform reconstruct what was said. The snapshot is gated by active-dispute status; it is not taken on every deletion.
  • Tenant-owned notes are deliberately handled differently. CCPA gives the customer rights to their own data, not to data the tenant authored about them in a business-records capacity. OPERATOR CONFIRM: This stance should be reviewed with legal counsel before launch.
  • The §17.10 deletion grace period (30 days) currently doesn't distinguish "soft delete that can be reversed" from "deletion in progress." During the 30-day grace, messages.content is still readable; only after grace does the NULL-out happen.

25.5 Sub-Processors ¶

Disclosed in privacy policy; updated annually:

  • Anthropic (AI inference)
  • OpenAI (embeddings only; no chat content)
  • Supabase (database, auth, storage)
  • Vercel (hosting, edge functions)
  • Stripe (payments, Connect, 1099 reporting)
  • Resend (email delivery)
  • Inngest (background job processing)
  • Sentry (error tracking, PII-scrubbed)

25.6 Data Residency ¶

US-only at launch. All data centers in US. Architecture supports EU residency for GDPR additions later (separate Supabase regions, regional routing in main app).

25.7 Consent Granularity ¶

Distinct consents per data use:

  • Account creation: required (per ToU)
  • AI conversation processing: required (per AI Disclaimer)
  • Memory extraction: opt-out (default on; users.memory_opt_out flag disables)
  • Marketing email: opt-in (default off; users.marketing_email_opt_in)
  • Travel news email: opt-in (default off; users.travel_news_opt_in)
  • Performance analytics: opt-out (default on)

25.10 Production Data on Staging — Privacy Posture ¶

The staging environment receives a copy of the production database at the start of every release pipeline. PII is not anonymized in the staging copy. This is a deliberate trade-off documented in §30.9.1; it sacrifices a portion of the standard “test data is synthetic only” posture in exchange for E2E test fidelity against real data shapes and volumes.

Customer-facing implications:

  • Staging data is subject to the same retention schedule as production for the purpose of customer deletion requests. A CCPA deletion request must purge the customer from staging on the next release cycle (the prod copy that occurs after deletion will no longer contain the record).
  • Staging is included in breach response scope per §26.10. A breach affecting the staging database is reportable to the same extent as a breach of production.
  • Sub-processor disclosure (§25.5) accurately reflects staging data exposure: the same vendors hold or process staging data as hold or process production.

The controls preventing customer impact from staging activity are listed in §30.9.1 and §26.13.
